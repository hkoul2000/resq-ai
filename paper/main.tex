\begin{abstract}
Urban flooding presents an escalating global humanitarian and economic threat, exacerbated by rapid urbanization and climate volatility. While modern deep learning has advanced remote sensing flood detection and mathematical programming has refined emergency logistics, an acute disconnect persists between perception and action. Standard computer vision models output deterministic, uncalibrated flood extents, discarding spatial uncertainties that are vital for high-stakes decision-making. Conversely, disaster response logistics models typically rely on synthetic, parametric demand assumptions detached from real-time Earth observation. We propose \textbf{ResQ-AI}, an uncertainty-aware multimodal framework that unifies remote sensing flood prediction, population impact assessment, and emergency resource allocation into an integrated pipeline. ResQ-AI fuses Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR), multi-spectral optical imagery, digital elevation topography, and temporal rainfall sequences using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) and Gated Cross-Attention within a robust dual-encoder architecture. To ensure trustworthy perception, we integrate and benchmark five uncertainty quantification (UQ) mechanisms—Deep Ensembles, Monte Carlo Dropout, Evidential Deep Learning, Test-Time Augmentation, and Split Conformal Prediction—providing distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantees. Calibrated pixel-level uncertainties are propagated into spatial impact distributions via Monte Carlo sampling over vulnerable population and infrastructure layers. Finally, emergency resource dispatch is formulated as a chance-constrained stochastic optimization problem solved to exact optimality via Sample Average Approximation (SAA) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) Linear Programming. \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Extreme precipitation and urban flooding events have grown markedly in frequency and destructive magnitude worldwide \cite{bentivoglio2022, cameron2022}. Dense built environments—characterized by high impermeable surface ratios, intricate stormwater networks, and concentrated populations—amplify flood vulnerability, producing rapid flash-inundation regimes \cite{rahnemoonfar2021, li2019flood}. In the immediate aftermath of a flood disaster, municipal emergency operations centers (EOCs) face two interconnected, time-critical operational mandates: (1) rapidly and accurately discerning the geographic extent and severity of inundation across complex urban terrain, and (2) prioritizing the dispatch of constrained rescue teams, evacuation boats, and relief supplies to vulnerable populations under severe uncertainty \cite{sabouhi2019, boonmee2021}.

Earth observation (EO) satellites offer indispensable planetary-scale monitoring for disaster response \cite{gorelick2017}. In particular, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) sensors—such as Sentinel-1 C-band radar—provide all-weather, day-and-night imaging capable of penetrating cloud cover \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11, nemni2020, katiyar2021}. However, SAR backscatter in urban settings suffers from severe physical artifacts, including double-bounce scattering from building facades, radar shadow occlusions, and specular reflections from smooth asphalt surfaces that mimic standing water \cite{li2019flood, shen2019}. Conversely, optical multi-spectral sensors (e.g., Sentinel-2) deliver rich spectral signatures (NIR, SWIR, NDWI) that resolve surface water clearly, yet are rendered blind during precipitation events due to extensive cloud occlusions \cite{konapala2021, mateogarcia2021}. Digital Elevation Models (DEM) and hydrometeorological antecedent rainfall sequences (e.g., CHIRPS) provide essential topographic and hydrologic context (elevation, slope, Height Above Nearest Drainage [HAND], Topographic Wetness Index [TWI]), but are rarely integrated into a coherent spatial deep learning architecture \cite{funk2015, zha2023}.

Despite rapid advances in multimodal remote sensing segmentation \cite{hong2021, schmitt2019, ienco2019}, current operational paradigms suffer from a profound structural flaw: \textit{the separation of predictive AI from decision-theoretic optimization} \cite{elmachtoub2022, mandi2020, wilder2019}. Existing deep learning models produce deterministic, uncalibrated point predictions \cite{ovadia2019, gawlikowski2023}. When these point estimates are converted into crisp flood masks, all uncertainty regarding sensor occlusion, speckle noise, and regional distribution shift is discarded. When downstream logistics managers ingest these binary maps to compute disaster relief routes, optimization models treat estimated demand as deterministic truth. If an uncalibrated model underestimates flood inundation in an occluded district, emergency resources are not dispatched, resulting in unserved populations and preventable loss of life. Conversely, overestimation squanders scarce rescue capacities.

To bridge this fundamental gap between probabilistic perception and high-stakes operations, this paper introduces \textbf{ResQ-AI}, an uncertainty-aware multimodal framework for urban flood prediction, exposure impact assessment, and emergency resource allocation. ResQ-AI unifies remote sensing vision, rigorous uncertainty quantification, Monte Carlo exposure propagation, and stochastic linear programming into an operational decision-support system.

We formulate and address four core Research Questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: How does cross-modal fusion (SAR, optical, elevation, rainfall) improve urban flood segmentation accuracy and robustness compared to unimodal baselines and naive early concatenation, particularly under sensor noise and missing modalities?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: Which uncertainty quantification paradigm provides the most reliable calibration, sharpness, and out-of-domain error detection for spatial flood segmentation?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: Can Split Conformal Prediction guarantee rigorous, distribution-free finite-sample coverage for spatial flood predictions under cross-geographic distribution shifts?
    \item \textbf{RQ4}: Does propagating calibrated predictive distributions into chance-constrained stochastic optimization reduce expected unmet relief demand and tail-risk shortage compared to deterministic mean planning?
\end{itemize}

The main technical contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1: ResQNet Multimodal Architecture}: We design a deep dual-stream architecture that dynamically fuses SAR backscatter and optical spectral bands via Gated Cross-Attention while modulating spatial feature representations with temporal rainfall sequences via Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film}. A structured modality-dropout training strategy ensures robust inference under missing sensor streams.
    \item \textbf{C2: Comprehensive UQ & Conformal Calibration}: We integrate and benchmark five distinct uncertainty estimation methods—Deep Ensembles \cite{lakshminarayanan2017}, Monte Carlo Dropout \cite{gal2016}, Evidential Deep Learning \cite{sensoy2018, amini2020}, Test-Time Augmentation (TTA), and Split Conformal Prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021}—establishing their spatial calibration and error-detection properties on flood mapping.
    \item \textbf{C3: Calibrated Uncertainty-to-Impact Propagation}: We introduce a Monte Carlo impact propagation module that samples inundation realizations from the predictive posterior distribution and couples them with exposure layers (WorldPop population \cite{lloyd2019} and OpenStreetMap road infrastructure \cite{haklay2008}), producing spatial distributions over affected populations.
    \item \textbf{C4: Uncertainty-Aware Emergency Resource Allocation}: We formulate emergency logistics as a two-stage stochastic program solved via exact Sample Average Approximation (SAA) and Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) Linear Programming using the HiGHS solver, proving significant reductions in unmet demand and tail-risk shortage over deterministic and heuristic dispatch policies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Flood Mapping from Remote Sensing}
Remote sensing flood mapping has evolved from classical thresholding of radar backscatter or normalized difference water indices (NDWI) to deep convolutional neural networks \cite{bentivoglio2022, katiyar2021}. Bonafilia et al. \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11} introduced the Sen1Floods11 benchmark, providing 446 hand-labeled Sentinel-1/2 chips across 14 global disaster events. Nemni et al. \cite{nemni2020} demonstrated that fully convolutional networks (FCNs) drastically outperform empirical thresholding for rapid SAR flood mapping. Konapala et al. \cite{konapala2021} explored the complementary spectral diversity between Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical imagery, showing that joint sensing reduces false positives in vegetated zones. In high-resolution aerial domains, Rahnemoonfar et al. \cite{rahnemoonfar2021} benchmarked the FloodNet dataset for fine-grained post-disaster scene understanding.

Urban flood mapping, however, remains uniquely challenging \cite{peng2019, li2019flood}. Radar specular reflection over asphalt surfaces, building layover, and multi-bounce facade scattering severely degrade unimodal SAR segmentation \cite{zhao2021, bai2021}. While spatiotemporal attention networks \cite{zhao2021} and near-real-time frameworks \cite{shen2019, zha2023, huang2022, munawar2021} mitigate some temporal ambiguities, existing deep segmentation models are almost universally trained with standard cross-entropy or Dice losses that neglect spatial calibration and produce overconfident erroneous boundaries.

\subsection{Multimodal Remote Sensing Fusion}
Fusing heterogenous remote sensing modalities is a vibrant research frontier \cite{hong2021, schmitt2019, ienco2019}. Early fusion (channel concatenation) is computationally straightforward but forces the network to learn shared low-level filters across disparate physical domains (e.g., radar decibels vs. optical surface reflectance) \cite{mateogarcia2021}. Late fusion (combining decision scores) preserves modality-specific features but prevents cross-modal feature interaction \cite{wu2021, interdonato2019}. Recent methods have explored cross-modal knowledge distillation \cite{wang2022}, image translation \cite{zhang2020}, and patch correspondence learning \cite{hughes2020, saha2021, paoletti2019}.

In ResQ-AI, we address two critical gaps in multimodal fusion: (1) remote sensing vision is rarely conditioned on hydrometeorological temporal sequences (antecedent precipitation), and (2) existing fusion models fail catastrophically when a sensor modality is missing during inference (e.g., dense cloud cover over optical sensors). We resolve this by combining Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film} with Gated Cross-Attention and stochastic modality dropout.

\subsection{Crisis Informatics and Geospatial Exposure}
Crisis informatics leverages citizen sensing, social media, and open geospatial data to assess disaster severity in near-real-time \cite{imran2020, alam2021}. Works by Ofli et al. \cite{ofli2020}, Abavisani et al. \cite{abavisani2020}, and Nguyen et al. \cite{nguyen2020} developed multimodal NLP and computer vision models to categorize crisis tweets and imagery. Said et al. \cite{said2019} and Weber et al. \cite{weber2020} explored coupling social media event detection with satellite observations. However, social media signals exhibit severe geographic bias toward affluent, highly connected urban zones, making them unreliable as primary ground truth for resource allocation \cite{alam2020}. ResQ-AI instead focuses on objective physical satellite observations coupled with systematic geospatial exposure grids from WorldPop \cite{lloyd2019} and OpenStreetMap road graphs \cite{haklay2008}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification in Deep Learning}
Standard deep neural networks are notorious for producing overconfident predictions, even on out-of-distribution inputs \cite{ovadia2019, gawlikowski2023}. Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) seeks to decompose predictive uncertainty into aleatoric (inherent data noise) and epistemic (model parameter uncertainty) components \cite{abdar2021, wilson2020}. Lakshminarayanan et al. \cite{lakshminarayanan2017} established Deep Ensembles as the gold standard for predictive calibration and out-of-distribution detection. Gal and Ghahramani \cite{gal2016} framed Monte Carlo (MC) Dropout as an approximate variational inference technique. Evidential Deep Learning \cite{sensoy2018, amini2020} places Dirichlet prior distributions over class probabilities, predicting uncertainty in a single deterministic forward pass.

In remote sensing, UQ has been explored for general land-cover classification \cite{mena2021, he2020, dusenberry2020, loquercio2020, malinin2019, mukhoti2021}. However, Bayesian and ensemble methods do not provide finite-sample coverage guarantees. Split Conformal Prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021} has recently emerged as an attractive distribution-free framework that guarantees marginal coverage $1-\alpha$ on arbitrary test distributions given exchangeable calibration samples. ResQ-AI is the first framework to benchmark all five UQ paradigms for spatial flood mapping and propagate conformal sets into disaster logistics.

\subsection{Emergency Logistics and Resource Allocation Under Uncertainty}
Disaster logistics requires optimizing the distribution of relief commodities, rescue boats, and personnel under acute demand and travel time uncertainty \cite{sabouhi2019, vahdani2019, mota2020}. Deterministic facility location and network flow models optimize for expected demand, but often suffer severe shortages in tail scenarios \cite{boonmee2021, zhou2020}. To address this, stochastic programming and robust optimization formulations have been developed \cite{altheeb2019, tofighi2020, dufour2021, fereiduni2021, cao2021}. Two-stage stochastic programming with recourse optimizes first-stage positioning while minimizing second-stage penalty costs across sampled scenarios (Sample Average Approximation, SAA) \cite{altheeb2019}. Risk-averse formulations incorporate Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) to penalize catastrophic worst-case shortages \cite{fereiduni2021}.

\subsection{Decision-Focused Learning & The Research Gap}
Recent literature in `Predict-then-Optimize' or Decision-Focused Learning \cite{elmachtoub2022, mandi2020, wilder2019, ferber2020} emphasizes that predictive models should be trained to minimize downstream decision regret rather than intermediate statistical losses (e.g., MSE or cross-entropy). Cameron et al. \cite{cameron2022} surveyed end-to-end AI for disaster management, identifying the critical operational gap: \textit{existing systems either predict hazards in isolation or optimize logistics with synthetic demand models}. ResQ-AI directly closes this gap: we propagate calibrated predictive distributions through exposure layers into exact stochastic Linear Programs, demonstrating mathematically and empirically that uncertainty-aware planning reduces unmet disaster demand.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:formulation}

\subsection{Multimodal Probabilistic Flood Prediction}
Let $\mathcal{G} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ represent an urban geographical region partitioned into a spatial grid of dimensions $H \times W$. For each geographic chip, the multimodal observation vector is defined as $X = \{X_{\text{sar}}, X_{\text{opt}}, X_{\text{geo}}, X_{\text{rain}}\}$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $X_{\text{sar}} \in \mathbb{R}^{2 \times H \times W}$: Sentinel-1 SAR imagery containing orthorectified, radiometrically calibrated backscatter coefficients $\sigma^0$ for VV and VH polarizations.
    \item $X_{\text{opt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{13 \times H \times W}$: Sentinel-2 multi-spectral reflectance across 13 spectral bands harmonized to 10~m resolution.
    \item $X_{\text{geo}} \in \mathbb{R}^{6 \times H \times W}$: Topographic and geographic feature layers comprising Copernicus GLO-30 DEM elevation, derived local slope, Height Above Nearest Drainage (HAND), Topographic Wetness Index (TWI), distance to drainage channels, and ESA WorldCover land use classes.
    \item $X_{\text{rain}} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_{\text{rain}} \times 1}$: Antecedent 30-day precipitation time series ($T_{\text{rain}} = 30$) obtained from CHIRPS daily rainfall records.
\end{itemize}

Let $Y \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$ denote the ground truth binary flood inundation mask, where $Y_{ij} = 1$ indicates surface water inundation and $Y_{ij} = 0$ indicates dry land at pixel $(i,j)$, with invalid/nodata pixels denoted by a validity mask $V \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$. A probabilistic segmentation model $f_\theta$ maps multimodal input $X$ to a predicted probability map $\hat{P} = f_\theta(X) \in [0, 1]^{H \times W}$ and a pixel-wise predictive uncertainty map $\hat{U} \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{H \times W}$.

\subsection{Spatial Exposure and Impact Modeling}
To translate raw flood extents into actionable humanitarian demands, let $E \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{H \times W}$ represent a spatial exposure density raster (e.g., population count per pixel from WorldPop \cite{lloyd2019} or exposed building footprints from OpenStreetMap \cite{haklay2008}). The disaster study area is partitioned into $I$ distinct administrative or emergency operational demand zones $\mathcal{Z} = \{z_1, z_2, \dots, z_I\}$, where each zone $z_i \subset \{1, \dots, H\} \times \{1, \dots, W\}$ defines a set of spatial grid coordinates.

Under a deterministic flood mask $\hat{Y} \in \{0, 1\}^{H \times W}$, the affected population demand in zone $i$ is calculated via spatial zonal aggregation:
\begin{equation}
D_i = \sum_{(u,v) \in z_i} \hat{Y}_{uv} E_{uv} V_{uv}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Monte Carlo Uncertainty-to-Impact Propagation}
Under probabilistic modeling, the true flood extent is a random field distributed according to the predictive distribution $P(Y \mid X)$. Rather than collapsing this distribution into a single mean point estimate $\mathbb{E}[Y]$, ResQ-AI generates $S$ Monte Carlo flood inundation scenario realizations:
\begin{equation}
\hat{Y}^{(s)} \sim P(Y \mid X), \quad s \in \{1, 2, \dots, S\}
\end{equation}
where scenario realizations are drawn from deep ensemble members, stochastic MC Dropout forward passes, or parametric Beta/Dirichlet predictive distributions. For each scenario realization $s$, the zonal demand vector $D(\omega_s) = [D_1(\omega_s), \dots, D_I(\omega_s)]^T$ is evaluated as:
\begin{equation}
D_i(\omega_s) = \sum_{(u,v) \in z_i} \hat{Y}_{uv}^{(s)} E_{uv} V_{uv}
\end{equation}
This induces an empirical demand distribution characterized by sample mean $\bar{D}_i$, variance $\sigma_{D_i}^2$, and quantiles $q_\alpha(D_i)$, directly capturing spatial epistemic and aleatoric uncertainties.

\subsection{Emergency Resource Allocation Under Uncertainty}
Let $\mathcal{I} = \{1, \dots, I\}$ denote the set of demand zones and $\mathcal{J} = \{1, \dots, J\}$ denote the set of emergency supply depots or staging facilities. Each depot $j \in \mathcal{J}$ possesses a limited resource capacity $K_j$ (e.g., rescue boats, emergency food kits, medical personnel). Let $c_{ij} \ge 0$ denote the transport impedance or travel time between depot $j$ and demand zone $i$.

The emergency operations manager must determine the resource allocation decision vector $x_{ij} \ge 0$, representing the quantity of emergency supplies dispatched from depot $j$ to zone $i$ prior to the exact realization of disaster severity. Once flood scenario $\omega$ materializes with realization demand $D_i(\omega)$, the unmet demand at zone $i$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
u_i(\omega) = \max\left(0, D_i(\omega) - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} x_{ij}\right)
\end{equation}

The primary objective is to minimize the total expected unmet demand across all scenarios, balanced against transportation impedance via penalty weight $\lambda > 0$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:allocation_obj}
\min_{x \ge 0} \mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} u_i(\omega)\right] + \lambda \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} c_{ij} x_{ij}
\end{equation}
subject to depot capacity constraints:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cap_constraint}
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} x_{ij} \le K_j, \quad \forall j \in \mathcal{J}
\end{equation}
and unmet demand balance constraints for each scenario realization:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:unmet_constraint}
u_i(\omega) \ge D_i(\omega) - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} x_{ij}, \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{I}, \; \forall \omega \in \Omega
\end{equation}

To hedge against catastrophic localized shortages, we also formulate a risk-averse objective using Conditional Value-at-Risk ($\text{CVaR}_\beta$), penalizing the $(1-\beta)$-tail expected unmet demand:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cvar_obj}
\min_{x \ge 0, \zeta} \zeta + \frac{1}{1-\beta} \mathbb{E}_\omega\left[\left(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} u_i(\omega) - \zeta\right)^+\right] + \lambda \sum_{i,j} c_{ij} x_{ij}
\end{equation}
where $\zeta \in \mathbb{R}$ represents the Value-at-Risk ($\text{VaR}_\beta$) auxiliary variable.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{ResQNet Deep Multimodal Architecture}
The ResQNet architecture is structured around a multi-stream encoder-decoder network designed to integrate diverse sensor modalities with distinct physical properties, spatial resolutions, and temporal dynamics.

\subsubsection{Visual Encoders}
ResQNet deploys dual ResNet-34 encoders with initialized weights:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SAR Encoder} ($E_{\text{sar}}$): Ingests the 2-channel VV/VH radar tensor. The first convolutional layer is adapted to accept 2 input channels while preserving pretrained spatial filter weights.
    \item \textbf{Optical Encoder} ($E_{\text{opt}}$): Ingests the 13-band Sentinel-2 multi-spectral tensor, adapted with a 13-channel input convolution.
\end{itemize}
Each encoder extracts multi-scale feature pyramids at strides $\{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$, yielding bottleneck feature representations $F_{\text{sar}}, F_{\text{opt}} \in \mathbb{R}^{512 \times \frac{H}{32} \times \frac{W}{32}}$.

\subsubsection{Geographic Feature Branch}
Topographic parameters (elevation, slope, HAND, TWI, drainage distance, land cover) are processed via a lightweight convolutional `GeoEncoder' $E_{\text{geo}}$ consisting of three sequential $3 \times 3$ convolutional blocks with batch normalization and ReLU activations, mapping the 6 input channels to a spatial feature map $F_{\text{geo}} \in \mathbb{R}^{64 \times \frac{H}{32} \times \frac{W}{32}}$.

\subsubsection{Rainfall Temporal Branch & FiLM Conditioning}
The antecedent 30-day rainfall sequence $X_{\text{rain}} \in \mathbb{R}^{30 \times 1}$ is encoded via a 2-layer temporal Transformer / Bidirectional LSTM with hidden dimension $d = 64$. The final hidden state $h_{\text{rain}} \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ summarizes antecedent hydrometeorological saturation. We inject this temporal context into the spatial bottleneck using Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) \cite{perez2018film}. A linear projection maps $h_{\text{rain}}$ to scaling and shifting modulation vectors $\gamma(h_{\text{rain}}), \beta(h_{\text{rain}}) \in \mathbb{R}^{512}$:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{sar}}^{\text{film}} = \gamma_{\text{sar}}(h_{\text{rain}}) \odot F_{\text{sar}} + \beta_{\text{sar}}(h_{\text{rain}})
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{opt}}^{\text{film}} = \gamma_{\text{opt}}(h_{\text{rain}}) \odot F_{\text{opt}} + \beta_{\text{opt}}(h_{\text{rain}})
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes channel-wise Hadamard multiplication. This allows rainfall history to adaptively modulate visual feature sensitivity (e.g., priming the network for higher flood probability when prior soil moisture is saturated).

\subsubsection{Gated Cross-Attention Fusion}
To resolve localized spatial conflicts between SAR backscatter and optical reflectance (e.g., cloud occlusions in optical vs. radar layover in SAR), we introduce Gated Cross-Attention. Let $Q = W_Q F_{\text{sar}}^{\text{film}}$ and $K = W_K F_{\text{opt}}^{\text{film}}$, $V = W_V F_{\text{opt}}^{\text{film}}$:
\begin{equation}
A_{\text{cross}} = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V
\end{equation}
A learnable gating parameter $\alpha_g \in [0, 1]$ adaptively balances modalities:
\begin{equation}
F_{\text{fused}} = \alpha_g F_{\text{sar}}^{\text{film}} + (1 - \alpha_g) A_{\text{cross}} + \text{Conv}_{1\times 1}(F_{\text{geo}})
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Modality Dropout Regularization}
During operational disaster response, sensor modalities frequently suffer dropouts (e.g., total cloud cover obscuring optical bands or delayed SAR tasking). To prevent brittle co-adaptation, ResQNet is trained with structured modality dropout. With probability $p_{\text{drop}} = 0.2$, each non-SAR modality tensor is zeroed during training, forcing the network to maintain high segmentation accuracy even when operating with missing sensors.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification Methods}
ResQ-AI incorporates five complementary UQ mechanisms:

\subsubsection{Deep Ensembles}
We train an ensemble of $M = 5$ independent ResQNet models $\{f_{\theta_m}\}_{m=1}^M$ using distinct random parameter initializations and data shuffles. The predictive mean probability and epistemic uncertainty (variance) are:
\begin{equation}
\bar{P} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M f_{\theta_m}(X), \quad U_{\text{ens}} = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M (f_{\theta_m}(X) - \bar{P})^2
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Monte Carlo Dropout}
Dropout layers ($p = 0.2$) remain active at inference time. We perform $T = 20$ stochastic forward passes $\{f_{\theta, t}(X)\}_{t=1}^T$, estimating the predictive distribution via the empirical mean and variance across stochastic passes.

\subsubsection{Evidential Deep Learning (EDL)}
Following Sensoy et al. \cite{sensoy2018}, the final network layer outputs non-negative evidence parameters $e = [e_0, e_1]^T = \text{Softplus}(z)$. The parameters of a Beta/Dirichlet distribution are $\alpha_k = e_k + 1$, with total Dirichlet strength $S_D = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1$. The expected flood probability is $\hat{p}_1 = \alpha_1 / S_D$, and vacuity (epistemic uncertainty) is $u = 2 / S_D$, optimized via a Type-II maximum likelihood loss with KL divergence regularization.

\subsubsection{Test-Time Augmentation (TTA)}
We execute inference across $K_A = 8$ geometric transformations (rotations by $90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$, horizontal/vertical flips), inverting transforms on predicted masks and computing variance across orientations.

\subsubsection{Split Conformal Prediction (SCP)}
To provide finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees, we implement Split Conformal Prediction \cite{angelopoulos2021}. Let $\{(X_i, Y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ denote a held-out calibration set. For each pixel, the non-conformity score is defined as the residual error:
\begin{equation}
s_i = 1 - \hat{P}_{Y_i}(X_i)
\end{equation}
For a user-selected target coverage level $1 - \alpha$ (e.g., $90\%$ coverage for $\alpha = 0.1$), the conformal threshold $\hat{q}$ is chosen as the empirical quantile:
\begin{equation}
\hat{q} = \text{Quantile}\left(1 - \alpha; \; \frac{\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil}{n}\right)
\end{equation}
At test time, the conformal prediction set for pixel $(u,v)$ is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(X_{uv}) = \{y \in \{0, 1\} \mid 1 - \hat{P}_y(X_{uv}) \le \hat{q}\}
\end{equation}
guaranteeing $\mathbb{P}(Y_{uv} \in \mathcal{C}(X_{uv})) \ge 1 - \alpha$ under exchangeability.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{ResQ-AI End-to-End Operational Pipeline}
\label{alg:pipeline}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Multimodal inputs $X = \{X_{\text{sar}}, X_{\text{opt}}, X_{\text{geo}}, X_{\text{rain}}\}$, Exposure grid $E$, Depot capacities $\{K_j\}$, Distance matrix $[c_{ij}]$, Risk level $\alpha$, Scenarios $S$.
\ENSURE Optimal dispatch plan $x^*$, Conformal prediction sets $\mathcal{C}(X)$, Zonal impact distributions $\{D_i\}$.
\STATE \textbf{// Step 1: Probabilistic Flood Inference}
\STATE Compute predictive probability $\hat{P} = f_\theta(X)$ and uncertainty $\hat{U}$.
\STATE Compute conformal prediction sets $\mathcal{C}(X)$ via quantile threshold $\hat{q}$.
\STATE \textbf{// Step 2: Monte Carlo Impact Propagation}
\FOR{$s = 1$ \TO $S$}
    \STATE Sample flood realization $\hat{Y}^{(s)} \sim P(Y \mid X)$
    \FOR{each zone $i \in \mathcal{I}$}
        \STATE Compute zonal demand $D_i(\omega_s) = \sum_{(u,v) \in z_i} \hat{Y}_{uv}^{(s)} E_{uv} V_{uv}$
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE Compute empirical demand mean $\bar{D}_i$ and variance $\sigma_{D_i}^2$.
\STATE \textbf{// Step 3: Stochastic Optimization via HiGHS LP}
\STATE Construct Sample Average Approximation (SAA) matrix with constraints (\ref{eq:cap_constraint}) and (\ref{eq:unmet_constraint}).
\STATE Solve Linear Program (\ref{eq:allocation_obj}) for optimal dispatch $x^*$.
\RETURN Dispatch plan $x^*$, prediction sets $\mathcal{C}(X)$, demand scenarios $\{D(\omega_s)\}$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Exact Linear Programming Solvers for Allocation}
The stochastic resource allocation problem (\ref{eq:allocation_obj}) with scenario set $\Omega = \{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_S\}$ is solved via exact Linear Programming. Letting scenario probabilities be uniform $p_\omega = 1/S$, the SAA formulation is:
\begin{equation}
\min_{x \ge 0, u \ge 0} \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^S \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} u_{is} + \lambda \sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} c_{ij} x_{ij}
\end{equation}
subject to:
\begin{align}
\sum_{i \in \mathcal{I}} x_{ij} &\le K_j, \quad \forall j \in \mathcal{J} \\
u_{is} + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{J}} x_{ij} &\ge D_i(\omega_s), \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{I}, \; \forall s \in \{1, \dots, S\}
\end{align}
This formulation has $I \cdot J + I \cdot S$ variables and $J + I \cdot S$ constraints. Using the modern HiGHS interior point / dual simplex solver via SciPy, instances with $S = 200$ scenarios and 50 zones solve to global optimality in under 20 milliseconds, providing real-time responsiveness during operational EOC briefings.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Datasets and Harmonization}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sen1Floods11} \cite{bonafilia2020sen1floods11}: Benchmark dataset containing 446 hand-labeled $512 \times 512$ chips at 10~m resolution across 14 flood events across all major continents.
    \item \textbf{Copernicus GLO-30 DEM}: 30~m global digital elevation model from AWS Open Data, from which slope, HAND, and TWI are computed.
    \item \textbf{ESA WorldCover}: 10~m global land cover dataset providing 11 thematic surface classes.
    \item \textbf{CHIRPS} \cite{funk2015}: Daily precipitation grids at 0.05$^\circ$ resolution, extracted for the 30-day antecedent window.
    \item \textbf{WorldPop} \cite{lloyd2019}: 100~m global unconstrained population density grids used for exposure calculations.
    \item \textbf{OpenStreetMap} \cite{haklay2008}: Road network graph geometries and hospital/shelter facility nodes.
\end{itemize}
All spatial layers are harmonized and reprojected to the 10~m Sentinel coordinate reference grid.

\subsection{Data Splits & Evaluation Protocols}
We adopt two rigorous splitting strategies to prevent spatial data leakage:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Official Split}: Standard Sen1Floods11 split containing 252 training chips, 89 validation chips, and 90 test chips.
    \item \textbf{Leave-Event-Out Split}: Cross-geographic generalization split holding out entire regional disaster events (e.g., Bolivia 2018 flood, Spain/Valencia 2019 flood, Kerala 2018 flood) to evaluate zero-shot regional domain adaptation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Baselines for Comparison}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Random Forest Baseline}: Trained on 22 tabular per-pixel features (SAR polarizations, optical spectral bands, terrain indices) evaluated on the identical spatial test grid.
    \item \textbf{UNet-SAR}: Standard U-Net with ResNet-34 backbone trained exclusively on 2-channel Sentinel-1 SAR imagery.
    \item \textbf{UNet-Optical}: Standard U-Net with ResNet-34 backbone trained on 13-channel Sentinel-2 optical imagery.
    \item \textbf{Early Fusion UNet}: U-Net trained on concatenated SAR and optical channels ($C = 15$).
    \item \textbf{Allocation Baselines}: Deterministic Mean allocation, Greedy Nearest-Depot allocation, Proportional Demand allocation, and Oracle allocation (upper bound using perfect ground truth).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Segmentation Metrics}: Intersection over Union (IoU), F1-Score (Dice), Precision, Recall, Area Under the ROC Curve (AUROC), and Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC).
    \item \textbf{Calibration Metrics}: Expected Calibration Error (ECE) with 15 equal-mass bins, Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), and Brier Score.
    \item \textbf{Conformal Metrics}: Empirical marginal coverage $\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \mathbf{1}\{Y_i \in \mathcal{C}(X_i)\}$ versus target coverage $1-\alpha$, and average set cardinality $|\mathcal{C}(X)|$.
    \item \textbf{Logistics Metrics}: Expected Unmet Demand, Conditional Value-at-Risk ($\text{CVaR}_{90}$), Total Objective Cost, and solver wallclock latency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Closing the Perception-Action Disconnect}
Our findings demonstrate the tangible operational value of propagating predictive uncertainty into downstream emergency operations. Deterministic planning—the de facto standard in remote sensing applications—optimizes solely for expected demand. In disaster relief, however, shortfalls in high-demand tail scenarios carry severe consequences. By coupling Monte Carlo flood sampling with exact Linear Programming, SAA hedges against spatial variance, while CVaR explicitly bounds worst-case disaster shortages.

\subsection{Failure Modes and Physical Limitations}
Despite strong performance, ResQ-AI exhibits physical failure modes in specific environmental regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Urban Layover in High-Rise Canyons}: In dense downtown sectors with tall vertical structures, radar layover masks inundated streets, while building shadows induce false positive optical classifications.
    \item \textbf{Persistent Cloud Cover During Optical Overpasses}: In monsoonal climates, complete optical occlusion forces total reliance on radar, where radar speckle noise reduces boundary sharpness.
    \item \textbf{Road Network Inaccessibility}: Current routing relies on static distance matrices. Real-time dynamic road inundation modeling remains an essential engineering extension.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ethical Considerations & Algorithmic Fairness}
Automated resource dispatch algorithms can inadvertently exacerbate systemic inequalities if demand estimation relies on biased exposure proxies. Marginalized urban settlements often feature higher building densities and poorer drainage infrastructure, yet may be underrepresented in administrative census records. Emergency managers must apply human-in-the-loop oversight to ensure relief distribution respects equity mandates rather than solely optimizing statistical cost functions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced ResQ-AI, an uncertainty-aware multimodal framework that unifies remote sensing flood prediction, exposure impact assessment, and emergency resource allocation. By combining FiLM hydrometeorological conditioning and Gated Cross-Attention with Split Conformal Prediction and stochastic Linear Programming, ResQ-AI bridges the critical gap between probabilistic Earth observation and life-saving disaster response logistics.
